Surprise! Not a technical blog post for once. I like making latte art, but I use a non-conventional method without the use of an espresso machine. So I wanted to share both my setup and, at the end, a step-by-step guide on how I do it.

## Why I Lart

I recently finished my Master's in computer science, so now it's only logical that I become a barista. As some of you might know, I like to occasionally do some latte arts (for brevity I will be calling them _larts_ throughout the post). You might have seen them on some Discord server I'm on, or have gotten a picture shoved in your face at only the slight mention of coffee. But to be straight up front, I'm not like the _other_ coffee enthusiasts with the thousand different tools that look more like butt plugs than anything you want to even have near food, lab-grade scales, multi-thousand-dollar coffee grinders, and of course an espresso machine that costs more than a kidney. I ain't got the time for all that.

But, crucially, during an internship I did, they had a fancy espresso machine where I got a taste of the other side. The 'other side' refers to making larts of course. A highlight of each day was my daily morning and lunch cappuccinos where I had to activate 100% of my brain just to draw some white blob on my coffee. If I was lucky, it might've even looked like garlic. After the internship ended, I was suddenly missing something big from my morning routine. I didn't have an espresso machine at home, so I couldn't do any larts anymore. I needed some solution, but I also didn't want to sell my kidney just yet.

![Garlic Lart](/content/posts/lart/garlic.jpeg)

My coffee taste standards are absolutely in the gutter, which I proudly trained during the last quinquennium while slurping away at my daily free coffees at VIS. This means that I'm well suited to basically drink any quality of coffee, after all, it "muss ned schmegge, muss wirke". So I'm completely fine with sipping away on Nespresso coffee or using _pre-ground_ coffee powder. Frankly, I just bulk-buy one of the random Coop coffee powders in multiple kilos at a time.

## The Home-Setup

We already had a Nespresso machine and a fancy milk foamer at home. The milk foamer is the type where you can pour in some milk and then have it foam up the milk by a loooot on its own. Like when you make some hot chocolate and want some nice heavy foam on top. I tried using that contraption to initially create the foamed milk to use for a lart, but you could barely call it liquid, it was so foamy. There was absolutely no chance to use it for making any patterns.

So I looked into alternatives. One option was to use a milk-frother wand:

![Milk frother](/content/posts/lart/milk-frother.jpeg)

But I had little luck using that to get the right consistency and it also caused the milk to be riddled with bubbles (which makes it fairly unusable for larting). Next, I discovered the following "solution" which gives you access to a steam wand without having to buy a whole espresso machine, but let's be real, this is basically just a pressure-cooker bomb:

![Bellman Stovetop](/content/posts/lart/bellman-stovetop.png)

It's called the Bellman Stovetop, and no, I'm not going anywhere near that thing. But then, in one of my daily YouTube grind sessions, I stumbled upon a [YouTube video](https://youtu.be/wFFJ-NLjNZY) on how to foam up milk to the right consistency with a somewhat unconventional method: Just use a french press to pump the milk into the right shape.

I ordered myself a small plastic-glass french press which also allows me to throw it into the microwave. I got myself the 0.35l Bodum Kenya french-press. With this, I'm now able to get consistently a good level of foamed milk which is perfect to create larts.

![French Press](/content/posts/lart/french-press.jpg)

An additional hurdle I encountered in the beginning was that Nespresso machines are very badly suited for larts. The machines add some fake crema on top which makes it very hard for the milk to stay on top of the surface when you try to draw your pattern. I therefore got myself a little 2-cup Bialetti Moka pot. Moka coffee has absolutely zero crema, which makes it perfect for larting.

![Bialetti](/content/posts/lart/bialetti-empty.jpg)

**Note:** Bialettis don't work on induction. Only a few months later did I see that there's actually a Bialetti version specifically made for induction. But the current setup works, so not looking to change it right now.

I've now been using the Bialetti + french-press setup for the past two years to make my larts and haven't had any complaints yet.

## How-to Lart

Here's my quick guide on how I make my larts.

### 1. Get the Bialetti Cooking

The Bialetti takes the longest. I usually have it on 7/9 heat on the induction stove (not the most efficient transfer I guess) and it takes around 5 mins in total. There are thousands of different ways people prefer their moka to be done with all sorts of rituals. I just take the path of least resistance. A large part of the taste is masked by the milk anyway.

1. The moka pot should be water-clean beforehand. Don't need that musty coffee stuck to the sides. It's a myth that not cleaning your Bialetti will make it taste better.
2. Put water straight out of the tap into the base (don't care about the temperature).
3. Throw the coffee powder into the basket. Don't squish it!
4. Screw together the pot and let it heat up on 7/9 heat.
5. After a few minutes it starts boiling and the coffee starts coming out. I don't change anything.
6. Once the coffee starts spurting out, it's the signal that it's done (like in the picture). I close the cap and take it off the stove. I do not cool it down with water or anything. Used to cool it down, but honestly it doesn't matter that much.

![Bialetti Done](/content/posts/lart/bialetti-done.jpg)

I've come to realize that from the time I hear the Bialetti getting louder because of the water boiling, to the point where it's finished, it takes a bit more than a minute and a half. The water boiling is the signal for me to start heating the milk for step 2.

### 2. Get the Milk Ready

The milk needs to be warm (almost hot) to be foamable. I use cow milk, but [alternatives should also work](https://youtu.be/6Oe914Q_Axw) (personally never tried though). If the milk is cold you'll just be making some bubbles and the lart will go nowhere. You also want to make sure that you foam the milk when it's still very warm, and then use the foamed milk right when you're done foaming it. If you prepare the milk before the coffee is ready, the foam starts to fall apart and be less fluid.

1. I fill up my french-press halfway. Through trial and error, it's the amount I've noticed fits into my cappuccino cups after I foam it.
2. Then I stick it into the microwave for a minute and a half.
3. I now use the french press to initially pump all the way up and down 3 times. This creates some initial big bubbles that are also pushed into the milk.
4. Then I proceed to pump 100 times, while trying to not break the surface barrier too much. If you cross the surface barrier too much while pumping, it can bring too much air into the milk and make it too foamy. But if you only pump a small part or half of the milk, you'll also not get the correct consistency in the end.
5. Once done, I like to pour it into a metal jug which I've felt is better at pouring milk.
6. Lastly, I smash the metal jug against the counter a few times while giving it a few swirls, to get all the major bubbles out.

## Alternative to Coffee: A Chart!

But you don't _have_ to use or drink coffee. It also works with hot chocolate! I've had the best results by initially creating a very highly concentrated water+chocolate powder mix: I use the same amount of water as would be in an espresso shot and then just put three teaspoons of powder in. I preheat it in the microwave for a bit, both so I can mix the chocolate powder better and so it doesn't completely cool down the milk afterwards.
